I bought a 1982 Honda CB750 in pieces from a guy in a suburb I'd never heard of. He'd started the rebuild, gotten distracted, and wanted it gone. I drove home with the frame in my truck bed and the engine in two cardboard boxes. That was six months ago.

The plan was to have it running by February. I am writing this in April. The bike is not running.

## What I underestimated

The carburetors. Always the carburetors. The CB750 has four of them, they're forty-four years old, and every one had its own unique set of issues. Varnish buildup, dried-out gaskets, a pilot jet that had corroded into the body and required a drill to remove without destroying everything. I spent three weekends on carbs alone.

The electrical system was also optimistic about my timeline. The previous owner had done some wiring modifications that I can only describe as creative. There were wire nuts inside the headlight shell. Wire nuts. On a motorcycle. I pulled the entire harness and rebuilt it from scratch using a factory service manual I found as a scan on a forum that looks like it was last updated in 2003.

## What's actually done

The frame is powder coated. The engine is rebuilt — new rings, valves lapped, gaskets replaced throughout. The carbs are finally clean and rebuilt. The wiring is sorted. New brake lines, new cables, new chain and sprockets.

What's left: getting the engine to start and idle reliably, sorting a fuel leak at the petcock, and the cosmetic stuff. Tank, fenders, seat. The end is actually in sight now, which feels different from the last four months of "the end is in sight."

## What I've learned

Mostly patience. And how to use a multimeter. And that "simple" vintage bikes are only simple relative to modern bikes, which is a very different thing from actually being simple.

Also: buy a parts bike. Whatever you think you'll save by not having one, you will lose in shipping costs for individual components.
